Hey, welcome to Eventually Consistent. This is day 5 of 77, and today we're talking about Read replicas.

A read replica is a copy of your primary database that exists mainly to answer read queries. The primary still takes the writes—inserts, updates, and deletes—and those changes stream out to one or more replicas. Your application, or a proxy sitting in front of it, sends writes to the primary and routes selects to the replicas. From the app's point of view it still looks like one logical database; under the hood you have a small fleet of machines with a clear division of labor.

Why bother building that? Because most products read a lot more than they write. Product pages, feeds, dashboards, and search all hammer the database with selects. You can keep buying a bigger primary for a while—more central processing unit, more memory, faster disks—but eventually you want more read capacity without making every write wait on more machines. Replicas give you that horizontal scale for reads. They also make a handy warm standby if the primary dies, and a place to dump heavy reporting or analytics so those queries do not crush your live traffic.

Picture an online store running on Postgres. The primary records new orders and inventory changes. Two streaming replicas, using Postgres's built-in physical replication, sit behind a load balancer and serve product pages, search, and order history. The connection pool knows the rule: writes go to the primary, selects go to the replica pool. After someone places an order, the app either reads that brand-new row from the primary for a short window, or it checks the replica's log sequence number and waits until replication has caught up before trusting a replica read for that user.

The catch is replication lag. With asynchronous replication—the usual setup because it keeps writes fast—a replica can be seconds behind, or worse under load or across regions. A shopper who just checked out might hit a lagging replica and not see their order yet. That feels like a bug even though the system is doing exactly what you configured. Synchronous replication can wait for at least one replica to confirm before acknowledging the commit. That removes the surprise, but it slows every write and couples the primary's latency to the slowest replica in the sync set. You also pay twice for storage and for the network bandwidth that carries the change stream, and a bad query plan on every replica is still a bad query—you just multiplied the cost across more machines.

So when someone says we scaled reads with replicas, ask three follow-ups. How do you handle read-your-writes right after a mutation? How much lag do you tolerate before you page someone? And do analytics share those same replicas, or do they have their own so a giant report cannot starve the storefront? The topology under most of this is leader–follower replication, and the consistency clients feel when lag shows up is eventual consistency—both of which sit nearby on this list.
