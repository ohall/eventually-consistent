Hey, welcome to Eventually Consistent. This is day 23 of 77, and today we're talking about Resharding.

Yesterday we talked about rebalancing, which is moving existing pieces of data around between machines. Resharding is the bigger, scarier cousin. It's when the pieces themselves are wrong. Maybe you need more shards than you started with, or maybe the key you split your data on turned out to be a bad choice. Either way, you have to cut the data up differently while the system keeps running.

So why does this happen? Usually because you guessed. Back when the app was small, somebody picked eight shards and split users by the first letter of their last name, or by a hash of the user ID. That worked fine for two years. Then traffic grew tenfold, and the eight shards are all running hot. Or worse, one shard is on fire while the others are bored, because one giant customer, the celebrity account everybody follows, lives on a single shard. Adding more machines doesn't help if the data can't be split any finer. You have to reshard.

Here's what a careful online reshard looks like. Tools like Vitess, the sharding layer that came out of YouTube, follow roughly this recipe. First, you create the new shards alongside the old ones. Second, you start copying the existing data across, which is called a backfill. Third, while that copy is running, every new write goes to both the old layout and the new layout, or gets streamed over from the old shard's change log, so nothing falls behind. Fourth, you verify, comparing row counts and checksums between old and new. Then you cut over reads to the new shards, watch closely, and finally cut over writes and retire the old ones. MongoDB added a built in version of this in release five point oh, and Amazon's DynamoDB does a quiet version automatically, splitting partitions when they get too big or too busy.

Now, a quick failure story, because this is where it goes wrong. A team I'll describe in general terms ran a reshard over a weekend. The backfill was running, the double writes were on, and the dashboard said ninety nine percent copied. Someone flipped writes over to the new shards early to stay on schedule. But the change stream from the old shards was lagging by about ten minutes. Every write in that gap landed on the old layout after the switch, and never made it across. They spent the next week reconciling orders by hand. The lesson is simple. Never cut over on a percentage. Cut over when replication lag is zero and the checksums actually match.

So what's the trade-off? Resharding buys you capacity and even load, which is the whole reason you sharded in the first place. But the price is a long, delicate migration that can run for days, doubles your write load while it's running, and has a scary moment at the cutover. That's why experienced teams try to avoid needing it. They pick a shard count with lots of headroom, often hundreds or thousands of small logical shards mapped onto a handful of machines, so that growth becomes cheap rebalancing instead of expensive resharding. And they think hard about the shard key up front, because changing it later is the hardest kind of reshard there is.
