Hey, welcome to Eventually Consistent. This is day 27 of 77, and today we're talking about Failover.

Yesterday we talked about split-brain, the mess you get when two sides of a cluster both think they are in charge. Failover is the other half of that story: how a system notices that the component you were depending on has failed, and switches work to a standby so users keep getting service.

Failover means promoting a backup to take over for a failed primary. The backup might be a hot standby already applying the primary's writes, or a warm replica that needs a moment to catch up. Detection usually comes from missed heartbeats, failed health checks, or a consensus group noticing the leader is gone. Once the system decides the primary is dead, it should fence that old primary so it cannot come back and write, promote the standby, and redirect clients. That last step might be a virtual I.P. address that moves, a D.N.S. record that changes, or a load balancer that picks a new target.

Why does failover exist? Because something will fail. Disks die, kernels panic, a whole availability zone goes dark. If every outage waited for a human to notice and promote a replica, your recovery time objective, the maximum downtime you promised, would be measured in hours. Automatic failover exists so a database can recover in minutes instead of waiting for a page at three in the morning.

Here is a concrete example. You run Postgres with streaming replication and a manager like Patroni watching the cluster. The primary is in availability zone A, a synchronous standby is in zone B, and they share a virtual I.P. The primary's host loses networking. After a few missed health checks, the remaining nodes lose the leader lock in etcd. Before anyone promotes the standby, the manager tries to fence the old primary so it cannot accept writes if the network flickers back. Then the standby is promoted, the virtual I.P. moves, and application pools reconnect. Amazon's Relational Database Service does a similar dance across availability zones: detect the failure, promote the standby, remap the endpoint. If replication was synchronous, you probably did not lose committed transactions. If it was asynchronous, the last few writes that had not yet reached the standby are gone. Kubernetes does a lighter version: a failed pod is replaced, and controller leader election uses a lease that expires so another replica can take over. Promotion without fencing is how you get split-brain.

The main trade-off is speed versus safety. Fail over too quickly, and a brief network blip promotes a new primary while the old one is still alive. That is flapping, and it is a short path to dual writers. Fail over too slowly and you honor safety but blow your recovery time. There is a second axis: data loss versus write latency. Synchronous replication keeps the recovery point objective near zero, but every write waits for the standby. Asynchronous replication is faster for writes, and failover may drop the tail of the log. Automatic is what you want at three in the morning if fencing is solid. Manual is what you want when a false positive would be worse than a few extra minutes of downtime.

So failover is not just promote the replica. It is detect, fence, promote, and redirect, and it is only as safe as the fencing. Tune the timeouts against real failure detection, not hope, and remember that the standby you never tested is not a standby.
