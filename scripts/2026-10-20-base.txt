Hey, welcome to Eventually Consistent. This is day 11 of 77, and today we're talking about BASE.

BASE is an acronym, and it's a little bit of a chemistry joke. It stands for Basically Available, Soft state, and Eventually consistent. It's the deliberate opposite of ACID, which we'll cover tomorrow. Where ACID says every transaction must be correct and isolated right now, BASE says the system should stay up and answer, and it's okay if the copies of your data disagree for a little while.

Let's take the three pieces one at a time. Basically available means the system keeps answering requests even when some machines are down or can't reach each other. The answer might be a bit stale, or partial, but you get one. Soft state means the data on any given replica can change even when nobody is writing to it, because background replication is still catching up. And eventually consistent means that if writes stop, every replica will converge on the same value. Not instantly, but eventually.

So why does this exist? Think back to yesterday's episode on the CAP theorem. When the network splits, you have to choose between consistency and availability. Keeping many machines in perfect lockstep takes coordination, things like locks, two phase commit, and majority quorums. That coordination adds latency on every request, and it stalls when nodes can't talk to each other. In the two thousands, big web companies like Amazon and eBay decided that for a lot of their data, a brief disagreement was much cheaper than an outage. The idea got its name in an influential article from two thousand eight by Dan Pritchett, called BASE: An ACID Alternative.

Here's a real example. Amazon DynamoDB, by default, gives you eventually consistent reads. When you write an item, the write is acknowledged and copied to other replicas in the region, usually within about a second. If you read right away, you might get the old value. You can ask for a strongly consistent read instead, but it costs twice as much read capacity, and it isn't available on global secondary indexes. Or think about the like count on a social media post. If it says one hundred and four on your phone and one hundred and five on your laptop for a few seconds, nobody gets hurt, and the site stays fast for millions of people.

Now the main trade-off. BASE buys you availability, low latency, and easy horizontal scaling. You can add machines and keep serving through failures. But the complexity doesn't disappear. It moves into your application. You have to handle stale reads, like a user who updates their profile and then sees the old version. You have to resolve conflicting writes, and the simple approach, last writer wins, can silently throw away data. Smarter options like version vectors or conflict free replicated data types take real engineering. And you can't easily enforce rules like a bank balance never going below zero, or a username being unique, without bringing coordination back in.

A good rule of thumb: use BASE for shopping carts, feeds, counters, and product catalogs, where a little lag is fine. Use ACID for money, inventory reservations, and anything that has to be unique. Most real systems mix both.
